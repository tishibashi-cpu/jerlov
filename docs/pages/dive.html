Why the colour changes

Kd of the ten types on a log scale; the chosen type is drawn heavily, with a dot where its printed Kd is least. Dashed: the absorption of pure water alone (Pope & Fry 1997). At 675 and 700 nm the Kd Jerlov printed for types I to II lies below it, by up to 10 %. Austin & Petzold (1986) pointed this out and recommended replacing Jerlov's Kd; jerlov ships their values as the source austin1986 (its DATA.md section 3).

The light that is left lies where Kd is least, so its colour follows that window. Among the wavelengths Jerlov printed, Kd is least at 475 nm for I to II, 500 nm for III, 550 nm for 1C to 7C and 575 nm for 9C.

The red end is the water itself. At 650 nm pure water alone absorbs 0.34 per metre, and Kd is 0.36 to 0.76 per metre over the ten types: in the clearest water Kd at 650 nm is little more than what the water alone absorbs.

The blue end is what is in it. At 400 nm Kd is 0.028 per metre in type I and 2.4 in 9C, 86 times as much; at 650 nm only 2.1 times. Jerlov wrote of his coastal types (Marine Optics, 1976, p. 137):

“These northern water masses are characterized by a relatively high amount of yellow substance.”

In the model Williamson & Hollins (2022) fitted to types IB to 5C, what absorbs beyond pure water does so 5.4 times as strongly at 412 nm as at 550 nm in IB, and 4.3 times in 5C. With the blue taken from one side and the red from the other, the window, and the colour, move to green and then yellow and orange. The narrower it gets, the nearer the light is to one wavelength, and the further outside sRGB its colour lies.

Which part of each type's blue loss is dissolved matter, which phytoplankton and which other particles was not recorded with the types, and this page does not divide it.

What this page assumes

  • Kd is the surface type's all the way down. Real water changes with depth; jerlov's descend follows the typical profile of Williamson & Hollins (2023), which moves the PAR 1 % depth of type I from 162 m to 105 m. It is not used here.
  • Jerlov's Kd belongs to one sun. His tables do not say which; the surface spectrum here is for a sun 30° from the zenith, and Kd is not changed for it.
  • Only 380–700 nm count, the range where both the sky model and Kd are given here. The surface spectrum over the same range is the white.
  • Colours are what a camera white-balanced at the surface would record, not a prediction of what an eye adapted to the water sees. Many of them lie outside sRGB and are clipped.
  • No light scattered towards the viewer from the water in between (no haze), no reflection from the sea floor, no shade. The materials are lit only by downwelling light.
  • This shows light, not visibility. How far an object can be seen is a different question that this page does not answer.

Where the numbers come from

  • Kd: N. G. Jerlov, Marine Optics (Elsevier, 1976), Table XXVII, printed at 16 wavelengths from 310 to 700 nm and read at 1 nm from the dataset that interpolates it.
  • Sunlight just below the surface: Gregg & Carder (1990), Limnol. Oceanogr. 35, 1657–1675, doi:10.4319/lo.1990.35.8.1657, clear sky, sun 30° from the zenith, 21 June, visibility 10 km, and the standard atmosphere of their Table 5.
  • Pure water's absorption: Pope & Fry (1997), Appl. Opt. 36, 8710–8723, doi:10.1364/AO.36.008710.
  • Jerlov's Kd below pure water: Austin & Petzold (1986), Opt. Eng. 25, 471–479, doi:10.1117/12.7973845, section 7.
  • Absorption beyond pure water: the a of types IB to 5C and the pure water absorption it was built on, Williamson & Hollins (2022), as jerlov ships them (its DATA.md section 20).
  • Colour: CIE 1931 2° observer, sRGB (IEC 61966-2-1).
  • Materials: USGS Spectral Library Version 7 (Kokaly et al. 2017), CC0.
  • All inputs were taken from jerlov , the Python package that collects these sources. The page only multiplies and integrates; its colours and PAR values match jerlov's own spectrum_to_srgb and par_profile to within 0.00001, which a test in its repository checks.
