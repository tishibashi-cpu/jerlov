Where the sources disagree

Some things in ocean optics have stayed unsettled for decades: measurements that disagree with each other, or with what other measurements say they should be. The package jerlov does not settle them. That takes new measurements. What it can do is put numbers on how far apart the published values are, and on how much of the gap the conditions a source leaves unrecorded could explain. Six questions, a figure each.

Question 1

How much do the published absorption spectra of pure water disagree?

Pure water is one substance, so its absorption should be the same in every careful laboratory. Below about 450 nm it absorbs so little that the measurements are hard.

Found
  • Pope & Fry (1997) over Mason, Cone & Fry (2016), both measured in an integrating cavity: .
  • In pure sea water the spectra give remote-sensing reflectances up to 6.85 times apart at 380 nm and 2.62 at 443 nm. In Jerlov IB, whose own contents absorb more, only 1.12 to 1.34 times.
Not known

Which laboratory value is right below 450 nm. No newer laboratory measurement than Mason et al. (2016) was found. jerlov does not choose: every function that needs a pure water absorption takes it from the caller.

Absorption of pure water, 1/m, log scale. Each source as jerlov ships it; gaps are where a source gives no value.

Question 2

What happens to light scattered at less than 0.1°?

Most light scattered in the sea turns by only a small angle. Petzold's measurements of 1971, still widely used, start at 0.1°. Below it his report extends the curve by a power law.

Found
  • The extension holds of all the scattering at the eight stations (right).
  • Bogucki et al. (2004) measured below 0.1° and found scattering on turbulence, which depends on how the water is being mixed that day, of 1 to 10 per metre. Petzold's extension cannot contain it.
  • It hardly matters for daylight. Dropping the extension moves Kd by −0.63 to +0.13 %: light turned by so little goes on as if unscattered.
Not known

Question 3

Do measured IOPs give back Jerlov's own Kd?

Williamson & Hollins (2022) measured absorption a and scattering b in waters they grouped by Jerlov type. Jerlov printed how fast daylight fades in the same types, Kd. If the two describe the same water, a radiative transfer calculation given the first should give back the second.

A Monte Carlo ran their a and b for six types at twelve wavelengths. The sun, the shape of the scattering and the pure water, which neither source records, together move Kd by times.

Found

Everywhere but the blue end, the two sources agree within what those conditions can make. The outlined cells lie outside: IB is too high at 425 to 500 nm, and the coastal types too low at 425 to 450 nm.

Not known

Why Jerlov's 1976 coastal blue Kd are higher than both his own 1968 values and the waters later typed by them. Which of IB's six campaigns are right.

Question 4

Does the Monte Carlo give back HydroLight's Kd?

Question 3 rests on a Monte Carlo written for it. Solonenko & Mobley printed, beside their a and b, the Kd that HydroLight, the code most of the field uses, made of them. Given the same a and b, does the Monte Carlo give back that Kd?

Found
  • At 143 cells from 350 to 700 nm, the Monte Carlo's Kd is a median of HydroLight's, with of the cells within 10 %. The criterion written down before any run, a median within 0.95 to 1.05 and 90 % of the cells within 10 %, is met.
  • HydroLight's Kd lies at every cell within what the paper's unprinted conditions (the phase function, the sun, the depth) make of the Monte Carlo's: the other stations move it by -12.7 to +26.0 percent, the sun by -12.7 to +21.5.
Not known

Which of the remaining differences are HydroLight's average-particle phase function, its sun and its depth, and which the codes. The paper prints none of the three.

Question 5

Why does the shallow-water model miss two groups of runs?

Lee et al.'s (1998, 1999) model gives the remote sensing reflectance of shallow water from a, bb, the depth and the bottom's albedo, and was fitted to HydroLight. Against a Monte Carlo it is within 10 percent in of runs, and misses in two groups: where pure water carries most of the backscattering (), and over a black bottom 1 or 4 m down in turbid water ().

Question 6

Why does Morel et al.'s f/Q archive dip at a sun of 45°?

Morel, Antoine & Gentili (2002) computed f and f/Q, the factors that correct water-leaving radiance for the sun and the view, and fitted them as straight lines in 1 − cos θs. Their archive's f at 45 degrees lies up to 3.44 percent below the line between its own values at 30 and 60.

Found
  • The dip belongs to one run: the sun at 45 degrees and 0.03 mg m⁻³, at all 7 wavelengths, by 1.01 to 3.44 percent. No other run is out of line.
  • The authors' own later tables, which NASA distributes, do not have it, and neither does the f/Q table of EUMETSAT's OLCI processor.
  • The paper's own Fig. 13 already draws it. Its symbols hold all 60 of the archive's values; where the paper's straight lines put f(0)/f at 45 degrees and 0.03 mg m⁻³, at 560, 620 and 660 nm, no symbol stands within (right).
Not known

What went wrong in that run. The package keeps its values, marks them suspect, and warns when they are read.

Question 7

Why does Jerlov's Kd lie below the absorption of pure water in places?

A diffuse attenuation cannot be smaller than what the water alone absorbs, yet some of Jerlov's Kd for the clearest type are. Austin & Petzold (1986) pointed it out and recommended replacing them. Against Smith & Baker's absorption of pure water, type I is as low as 0.666 of it at 600 nm in 1968 and 0.862 at 700 nm in 1976.

Found
  • Rounding cannot do it: half a printed step moves Kd by at most 3.6 percent.
  • A broad filter can, at some wavelengths. Marshall & Smith (1990) give the attenuation a detector sees through a filter (their Eq. 37); with pure water's own absorption and 0–10 m, a 40 nm filter at 700 nm sees 0.853 of it, which reaches the 1976 value there (right). At 675 nm no filter up to 60 nm does.
  • Raman scattering, the other cause Marshall & Smith name, cannot do it over Jerlov's 0–10 m. By their own model, the Raman light lowers the Kd of pure water there by at most 0.59 percent at 675 and 700 nm; only deeper does it matter.
Not known

The widths of Jerlov's filters (he says only that they had “a broad band width”), and whether all his red values come from the top 10 m. What accounts for the value at 675 nm.

Lines: the attenuation of pure water a detector sees through a Gaussian filter, averaged over 0–10 m, over its absorption (Smith & Baker 1981), against the filter's full width at half maximum. Marks at the right: Jerlov's type I Kd over the same absorption, 1968 (hollow) and 1976 (filled), where below 1. A line that reaches a mark means a filter that wide alone can bring pure water down to that value.
